\begin{abstract}
Head movements are a primary observable of driver attention, and modern
cabins increasingly carry several synchronized cameras.  We study four-class
recognition of head-movement direction (left/right/down/up) from three cabin
views, reporting a systematic campaign of more than one hundred trained
models on a 22-driver cohort.  Our contributions are threefold.
(1)~\emph{Method}: a parameter-efficient multi-view architecture that keeps a
single Kinetics-pretrained video transformer frozen and learns only a small
cross-view token-attention module (11M trainable parameters, 4\% of one
finetuned backbone).  Under person-held-out evaluation it improves \mF{} by
$+6.3$ points over an equally pretrained single-view model and $+4.1$ over
trivial late fusion (both significant), halves the across-driver variance,
and under a nested, selection-free protocol its \micro{} advantage over the
single-view control grows to $+5.1$ points ($p{=}8.6\times10^{-12}$).
(2)~\emph{Measurement}: we document how two common protocol defects---a
video-level split that leaks driver identity into validation, and per-batch
averaging of epoch metrics---jointly fabricated a coherent but false
architecture narrative on this task; several ``ablation-validated''
conclusions, including our own earlier ones, reversed under a sound protocol.
(3)~\emph{Negative results}: an analytic head-pose feature stream carries
genuine, class-complementary signal in isolation, yet none of three injection
routes (token-, query-, or logit-level) survived robustness checks, and six
class-imbalance remedies failed to prevent rare-class collapse.  We release
the person-held-out protocol, paired statistical tooling, and all per-run
predictions to support measurement-first research on small-cohort driver
monitoring.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{D}{river}-monitoring systems (DMS) are becoming a regulatory
requirement in new vehicles, and head behaviour is among their most
informative observables: mirror checks, shoulder checks, and downward glances
toward devices are all expressed as short, directed head movements.  Compared
with gaze estimation, head-movement classification is robust to eyewear and
illumination and can be computed from standard cabin cameras.  Modern cabins
increasingly provide \emph{several} such cameras, raising a natural question:
how much does multi-view observation actually contribute to recognizing
driver head behaviour, and through which fusion mechanism?

Answering this question rigorously is harder than it appears, for reasons
that are structural to driver-monitoring research rather than incidental to
our study.  Cohorts are small---recruiting and recording drivers is
expensive---so models see only tens of identities.  Classes are heavily
imbalanced, because natural driving produces many more downward and leftward
glances than upward ones.  And each driver contributes many highly correlated
clips, so a split at the \emph{clip} or \emph{video} level leaks identity:
a model can score well by recognizing \emph{who} is moving rather than
\emph{how}.  These conditions conspire to make small-cohort studies
vulnerable to false conclusions that survive conventional ablation practice.

This paper is deliberately \emph{measurement-first}.  Our initial campaign of
40+ runs, conducted under a conventional protocol (random video-level split,
default metric logging), produced what appeared to be a well-supported
progression of architectural improvements, each confirmed by ablations.
Auditing the protocol invalidated essentially all of it.  We rebuilt the
evaluation around person-held-out cross-validation with paired statistical
tests, re-ran every claim, and report what survives---together with a
controlled account of how the defective protocol manufactured the earlier
narrative, and with the negative results of our attempts to exploit head-pose
priors and to mitigate rare-class collapse.

Concretely, our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{\mvmin{}}, a minimal multi-view architecture: one frozen
Kinetics-pretrained ViViT encoder shared across views, a per-view token
compression to 17 tokens, and a small trainable transformer whose attention
spans views.  Under person-held-out evaluation it delivers significant
\mF{} and robustness gains over equally pretrained single-view and
late-fusion controls, and its advantage \emph{grows} under a stricter
nested protocol---the signature of a real effect rather than an artifact.
\item \textbf{A sound evaluation protocol} for small-cohort driver
monitoring---person-held-out $k$-fold with a nested selection-free variant,
epoch-accumulated metrics, and paired McNemar/bootstrap tests---released with
tooling, together with a quantified case study of how two silent protocol
defects fabricated a false state-of-the-art narrative.
\item \textbf{Documented negative results} on two fronts that the community
is likely to attempt: injecting analytic head-pose priors (the signal is
real, but no token-, query-, or logit-level route survived robustness
checks) and six remedies for rare-class collapse (all failed).  We argue
that identity-invariant representation, not fusion architecture, is the
binding constraint.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Driver monitoring and head behaviour}
Vision-based driver monitoring spans distraction and secondary-task
recognition~\cite{martin2019driveact,ortega2020dmd,abouelnaga2018distracted},
maneuver anticipation from cabin and road views~\cite{jain2015brain4cars},
and head-pose estimation as a gaze proxy~\cite{ruiz2018hopenet,zhou2020whenet}.
Fine-grained benchmarks such as Drive\&Act~\cite{martin2019driveact} and
DMD~\cite{ortega2020dmd} established multi-camera cabins as the standard
setting and report cross-subject splits, but the interaction between tiny
cohorts, class imbalance, and identity leakage has received little dedicated
analysis.  Our task---direction classification of spontaneous head
movements---is narrower than activity recognition but isolates exactly the
attention-relevant behaviour, and our cohort size (22 drivers) is typical of
the regime where measurement pitfalls dominate.

\subsection{Video backbones and parameter-efficient transfer}
Video classification progressed from 3-D CNNs~\cite{carreira2017i3d,
hara2018spatiotemporal,feichtenhofer2019slowfast} to transformer
architectures~\cite{bertasius2021timesformer,arnab2021vivit,tong2022videomae}
pretrained on large corpora such as Kinetics~\cite{kay2017kinetics}.  For
small downstream datasets, freezing the backbone and training light adapters
is often preferable to full finetuning~\cite{houlsby2019adapters,
lin2022frozenclip}.  Our results give this practice unusually sharp support:
on 80 training videos, partial unfreezing contributed nothing measurable,
while the pretrained representation contributed the single largest accuracy
gain of the study.

\subsection{Multi-view fusion}
Multi-view action recognition classically fuses at the input, feature, or
decision level~\cite{shahroudy2016ntu}.  Attention-based token fusion lets
each view's tokens attend to the others', subsuming learned per-view
weighting.  Our contribution is not a new fusion operator but a controlled
attribution: with backbone and pretraining held fixed, we measure what
cross-view attention buys \emph{beyond} late fusion on a small cohort---class
balance and across-driver robustness rather than raw accuracy.

\subsection{Evaluation pitfalls and negative results}
Dataset bias and leakage are recognized threats to empirical
validity~\cite{torralba2011bias,kapoor2023leakage}, and subject-dependent
splits are known to inflate results in wearable activity
recognition~\cite{jordao2018har}.  Statistical practice for classifier
comparison is likewise well established~\cite{mcnemar1947,dietterich1998,
efron1993bootstrap} yet rarely applied in applied vision papers.  We add a
documented, quantified case in driver monitoring where two defects interacted
to produce a self-consistent false narrative, and we normalize the reporting
of negative results~\cite{pineau2021reproducibility} for methods the
community would plausibly retry.

\section{Task and Dataset}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Recording and annotation}
Each of 22 drivers was recorded in four illumination conditions
(day/night $\times$ high/low ambient light), yielding 88 sessions.  Three
synchronized cameras (front, left, right; $332{\times}224$\,px) observe the
cabin.  Timeline annotations mark head-movement segments with one of eight
direction labels, merged to four classes---\emph{left}, \emph{right},
\emph{down}, \emph{up}---with vertical components taking precedence
(\eg{} left-up $\rightarrow$ up).  Unlabelled spans are excluded (no
background class).  Segment-level class frequencies are imbalanced:
down 43\%, left 28\%, right 21\%, up 9\% (pooled over held-out folds).

Three annotators labelled every session.  Frame-level inter-annotator
agreement is 91.0\% overall (399k co-labelled frames) and 94\% for the
\emph{right} class.  This matters for interpreting our results: the
rare-class failures reported later cannot be attributed to label ambiguity.

A SAM-3D body estimator provides 70 keypoints (2-D and 3-D) per frame per
view, of which eight are head-related (nose, eyes, ears, acromions, neck).

\subsection{Why this task is statistically hostile}
Three properties interact.  (i)~\emph{Small cohort}: 22 identities upper-bound
what any split can test.  (ii)~\emph{Correlated clips}: each session yields
tens of segments of the same person in the same seat, so clip- or video-level
splits measure identity recall.  (iii)~\emph{Imbalance}: the majority class
alone achieves 42.7\% accuracy.  A single 8-video validation split---the
protocol of our own first campaign---carries a binomial 95\% CI of
$\pm6.4$ points and, as we later measured, up to 15.6 points of across-fold
spread.  Every architectural effect we chased in four iterations was smaller
than that noise.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
We treat the protocol as a first-class contribution and specify it fully.

\textbf{Person-held-out 5-fold CV.}  The 22 drivers are partitioned into five
folds (4--5 drivers each, seeded).  For fold $k$, all sessions of the fold's
drivers form the evaluation set; the model trains on the remaining drivers.
Pooling folds scores every driver exactly once with a model that never saw
them ($n{=}3954$ segments).

\textbf{Nested selection-free variant.}  Checkpoint selection itself can leak:
choosing the best epoch on the same people one reports on inflated our arms
by $+0.9$ to $+2.0$ points (measured).  In the nested protocol, three drivers
are further carved out of each training set as an inner validation set;
checkpoints are selected on the inner set and reported on the held-out fold.
Train, inner-validation, and test sets are person-disjoint by construction.

\textbf{Metrics.}  \micro{} (segment accuracy), macro-accuracy, and \mF{},
all accumulated over the full epoch and reduced once.  We report \mF{}
alongside \micro{} because the majority class alone reaches 42.7\% \micro{};
balance is the operationally relevant axis for a monitoring system.

\textbf{Statistics.}  All model comparisons are \emph{paired} on identical
segment sets (verified fold-by-fold before testing): McNemar's test with
continuity correction~\cite{mcnemar1947,dietterich1998} for \micro{}, and a
paired bootstrap (1{,}500--2{,}000 resamples, 95\% CIs)~\cite{efron1993bootstrap}
for all metrics.  The pooled protocol's binomial CI is $\pm1.6$ points;
we treat sub-CI differences as noise regardless of nominal $p$-values.

\textbf{Training.}  All arms: 50 epochs, no early stopping, Adam
(lr $10^{-4}$, cosine decay), effective batch 16, bf16 mixed precision,
class-frequency weights in the cross-entropy loss, one H100 per run.
ViViT arms sample 32 frames at $224^2$; 3-D CNN arms 8 frames.

\section{Method: \mvmin{}}
\label{sec:method}
The architecture that survives evaluation is deliberately minimal
(Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}).  Let $V{=}\{\text{front},\text{left},\text{right}\}$
and let $x_v\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times T\times H\times W}$ be view $v$'s clip.

\textbf{Frozen shared encoder.}  A single ViViT-B/16$\times$2
encoder~\cite{arnab2021vivit}, pretrained on
Kinetics-400~\cite{kay2017kinetics}, processes each view independently with
shared (frozen) weights, producing token matrices
$Z_v\in\mathbb{R}^{(1+N_tN_s)\times D}$ with $N_t{=}16$ temporal tubelets,
$N_s{=}196$ spatial patches, $D{=}768$.  The encoder runs under
\texttt{no\_grad}; no backbone parameter is updated.

\textbf{Token compression.}  Attention over $3\times3137$ tokens is wasteful
and, on 80 training videos, an overfitting surface.  Per view we keep
$1{+}N_t{=}17$ tokens: the CLS token and one spatially averaged token per
tubelet,
\begin{equation}
z_v^{(t)}=\tfrac{1}{N_s}\textstyle\sum_{s=1}^{N_s}Z_v^{(t,s)},\qquad
t=1,\dots,N_t .
\end{equation}

\textbf{Cross-view fusion.}  Each view's tokens receive a learned additive
view embedding $e_v\in\mathbb{R}^{D}$; the $3\times17{=}51$ tokens are
concatenated into one sequence and processed by a 2-layer, 8-head
transformer encoder~\cite{vaswani2017attention} whose self-attention spans
all views.  Mean pooling and a linear head produce the 4-class logits.

\textbf{Capacity.}  Trainable parameters: 11M (fusion module and head),
\ie{} 4\% of one finetuned ViViT-B; multi-view inference reuses the single
frozen encoder three times.

Three design decisions deserve emphasis because each contradicts what our
own leaky-protocol ablations had ``established'': the backbone is fully
frozen (partial unfreezing measured n.s.\ under the sound protocol),
no keypoint stream is included (Sec.~\ref{sec:keypoints}), and aggregation
is plain mean pooling (learned gating hurt in every protocol we tried).

Tables~\ref{tab:main} and~\ref{tab:paired} support three statements.

\textbf{(1) Pretraining dominates \micro.}  Replacing the 3-D CNN with a
Kinetics-pretrained ViViT adds $+5.5$ \micro{} points---the largest single
effect measured in the entire campaign.  No fusion mechanism adds \micro{} on
top of it under the standard protocol.  Attribution required the single-view
ViViT control; without it, the $+5.1$ of the full multi-view arm over the
3-D CNN baseline would have been misread as a multi-view contribution.

\textbf{(2) Cross-view attention buys balance and robustness.}  Against the
equally pretrained single-view and trivial late-fusion controls, cross-view
token attention is \micro-neutral but gains $+6.3$ and $+4.1$ \mF{}
respectively (both significant) and reduces the across-driver spread from
.134 to .090.  For a monitoring system, missing rare-but-critical movements
(\emph{up}: mirror checks) and failing on unfamiliar drivers are the relevant
failure modes; this is the axis on which multi-view observation demonstrably
helps.

\textbf{(3) A previously winning fusion reverses.}  TS-CVA mid fusion, the
best fusion of our leaky-protocol campaign (48.9 vs.\ 37.2 single view),
scores \emph{below} its own single-view baseline under person hold-out
(45.4 vs.\ 47.0).  Fusion conclusions drawn under identity leakage did not
merely shrink---they changed sign.

\section{Head-Pose Priors: Real Signal, No Robust Injection Route}
\label{sec:keypoints}
Head movements should be readable from head pose, and the scenario provides
per-frame 3-D keypoints.  This section documents why naive keypoint fusion
failed, evidence that the signal itself is real, and the failure of three
injection routes---results we report so that others need not rediscover them.

\subsection{Why raw keypoint streams fail}
The raw $70{\times}3$ tensor is dominated by hands: 42 of 70 rows (60\% of
input dimensions) are hand points whose frame-to-frame motion is $4\times$
that of the head, leaving the head with ${\sim}3\%$ of the input's motion
energy.  Coordinates are unnormalized camera coordinates, so the largest
variance directions encode \emph{who and where the driver is}---precisely the
identity signal a person-held-out model must ignore.  And the earlier design
compressed the trajectory into a single token competing with 51 video tokens.
The head signal never actually reached the model; the corresponding arms
measured n.s.\ accordingly.

\subsection{Analytic head-pose features}
We compute what the network was supposed to learn.  With $p^{(i)}_t$ the 3-D
position of keypoint $i$ at frame $t$, define the ear midpoint
$o_t{=}\tfrac12(p^{\mathrm{le}}_t{+}p^{\mathrm{re}}_t)$, ear axis
$\mathbf{e}_t{=}\overline{p^{\mathrm{le}}_t{-}p^{\mathrm{re}}_t}$, and facing
direction $\mathbf{f}_t$ as the nose direction orthogonalized against
$\mathbf{e}_t$ (bars denote unit normalization).  Head angles follow
analytically:
\begin{align}
\mathrm{yaw}_t &= \operatorname{atan2}(f_{t,x},\,|f_{t,z}|),\quad
\mathrm{pitch}_t = \operatorname{atan2}(f_{t,y},\,\lVert f_{t,xz}\rVert),
\nonumber\\
\mathrm{roll}_t &= \operatorname{atan2}(e_{t,y},\,\lVert e_{t,xz}\rVert).
\end{align}
The per-frame feature (33-D) concatenates the three angles, their first
differences (the label is a \emph{movement}---a derivative), the head-centroid
velocity, and the eight head points expressed in the local frame
$(\mathbf{e}_t,\mathbf{f}_t,\mathbf{e}_t{\times}\mathbf{f}_t)$; angles and
local coordinates are centred by their per-segment temporal mean, removing
each driver's resting posture (identity) while preserving the movement.

\subsection{The signal is real}
A 64-unit MLP on these features alone---no video---reaches 27.2 \mF{} on
unseen drivers with \emph{non-zero F$_1$ on every class}: right 0.24 and
up 0.12, where every video model scores ${\approx}0.03$ and $0.00$
(Table~\ref{tab:collapse}).  Within a clip, yaw spans up to $69^\circ$; the
prior is strong and complementary to the video models' failure modes.

\subsection{Three injection routes, none robust}
\textbf{Token-level.}  Per-frame pose tokens (one per frame per view,
projected to $D$ and tagged with a stream embedding) initially produced the
best \micro{} of the campaign (53.3, $+1.7$ over \mvmin{},
$p{=}8.9\times10^{-5}$).  Three pre-registered robustness checks demoted it:
seed sensitivity ($+1.7/+3.3/+0.4$; significant in 2 of 3);
\emph{reversal} under the nested protocol ($-2.5$, $p{=}3.2\times10^{-7}$),
showing part of the gain came from checkpoint selection on scored drivers;
and a representation control in which raw flattened keypoints in the same
per-frame token format captured most of the effect ($+1.1$, $p{=}0.021$),
leaving the analytic encoding itself at $+0.6$ (n.s.).
\textbf{Query-level.}  Using the pose trajectory as the cross-attention query
for pooling: $+1.3$ \micro{} ($p{=}0.004$) but \mF{}-neutral and not robust
under nesting (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).
\textbf{Logit-level.}  A pre-registered 50/50 probability average of the
pose-only probe and the video model degraded \micro{} by $9.1$ points
($p{=}5.4\times10^{-30}$): the probe is 22 points weaker overall, and the
equal-weight blend loses more on frequent classes than it recovers on rare
ones.  An $\alpha$-sweep shows a mild trade-off region near $\alpha{=}0.8$
(up-F$_1$ $0.02{\to}0.06$ at $\approx$flat \micro), which we report as
sensitivity only: selecting $\alpha$ on test folds would reintroduce
selection bias.

The open problem is precise: an injection mechanism that preserves the
probe's rare-class competence without surrendering the video model's
frequent-class strength.  Learning the blend on nested inner-validation
drivers is the legitimate next step.

Two failure modes define the actual difficulty of this task.

\textbf{Collapse.}  \emph{Right} and \emph{up} are essentially never
predicted by any video model (Table~\ref{tab:collapse})---the best model
answers 1 of 59 \emph{right} segments correctly in the worst fold---despite
94\% annotator agreement on \emph{right}.  Six remedies spanning loss design
(focal~\cite{lin2017focal}, balanced softmax~\cite{ren2020balanced},
class weighting~\cite{cui2019classbalanced}), input design (clip-mean
subtraction), and fusion design (pose tokens, logit fusion) all failed
(Table~\ref{tab:remedies}).

\textbf{Driver memorisation.}  Under person hold-out, the best checkpoint
lands at epoch 0--4 in \emph{every} arm: models begin memorising training
drivers almost immediately, consistent with the large across-driver spread
(fold-level \micro{} ranges from 21\% to 63\% for the 3-D CNN baseline).
Clip-mean subtraction, our direct attack on static appearance, removed useful
signal along with identity.  We conclude that identity-invariant
representation---not fusion architecture---is the binding constraint for
small-cohort driver monitoring, and nominate it as the field's priority.

\section{How a Defective Protocol Fabricated a Narrative}
\label{sec:pitfalls}
We document this because both defects are common, silent, and mutually
reinforcing.

\textbf{Defect 1: identity leakage.}  Our first campaign split at the video
level; all seven validation drivers also appeared in training.  Scores
measured recall of known drivers: the same 3-D CNN drops from 53.6\% (leaky)
to 47.0\% (person-held-out), and fold-level results vary by 15.6 points
depending on which drivers are held out.

\textbf{Defect 2: per-batch metric averaging.}  Epoch metrics were computed
per batch (size 2) and averaged---inflating \mF{} by up to 17 points
(0.52 logged vs.\ 0.35 true)---and, worse, \emph{inconsistently}: one trainer
family accumulated correctly, so cross-architecture comparisons mixed two
incompatible scales.

Together these produced a coherent and false progression
(54.0 $\rightarrow$ 56.9 $\rightarrow$ 58.3 ``state of the art''), each step
confirmed by ablations run under the same defects; an intermediate
``architectures are indistinguishable'' reading, drawn from the same leaky
split, was equally wrong.  Under the sound protocol the query-pooling effect
($+3.5$) vanished, the unfreeze-depth sweep became n.s., and the TS-CVA
fusion champion fell below its own baseline.  Nothing about these defects is
exotic---one is a dataloader default, the other a metric-API misuse---which
is precisely why we report the case quantitatively and release the protocol
as the antidote.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}
\textbf{What multi-view is for.}  On this task, multi-view observation does
not raise headline accuracy over a strong pretrained single-view model; it
raises class balance and cross-driver stability.  Deployments should choose
multi-view for robustness, not for accuracy benchmarks.

\textbf{Scope.}  Our cohort (22 drivers, one vehicle type, four illumination
conditions) matches the regime we analyse but limits external validity;
cross-dataset evaluation (\eg{} Drive\&Act~\cite{martin2019driveact},
DMD~\cite{ortega2020dmd}) is future work.  Segment-level classification with
oracle temporal boundaries sidesteps detection; an online system must also
localize movements.  The keypoint study depends on one 3-D body estimator;
better head-dedicated pose estimators~\cite{ruiz2018hopenet,zhou2020whenet}
may change the injection results.  Finally, our negative results are
task-scoped: we show these remedies fail \emph{here}, not everywhere.

\textbf{Recommendations for small-cohort DMS research.}
(1)~Split by person; report pooled person-held-out results with per-fold
spread.  (2)~Select checkpoints on person-disjoint inner validation.
(3)~Accumulate metrics over the epoch; verify metric parity across baselines.
(4)~Pair every claim with McNemar/bootstrap statistics on identical samples.
(5)~Report \mF{} beside \micro{} whenever a majority class exceeds a third of
the data.  (6)~Publish per-run predictions so later work can re-test claims.

\section{Conclusion}
Under person-held-out, statistically tested evaluation of more than twenty
experimental arms, the defensible findings on multi-view driver head-movement
recognition are:
(1)~Kinetics-pretrained video transformers dominate accuracy
($+5.5$ \micro{} over a 3-D CNN);
(2)~a frozen-backbone cross-view token-attention module with 4\% of the
trainable parameters of one finetuned backbone adds class balance
($+6.3$ \mF), halves across-driver spread, and, under the strictest
selection-free protocol, adds $+5.1$ \micro{} ($p{=}8.6\times10^{-12}$)
over its single-view control;
(3)~head-pose priors carry real, class-complementary signal but no injection
route yet survives robustness checks;
(4)~rare-class collapse and driver memorisation, not fusion architecture,
are the binding constraints---six remedies failed;
(5)~two ordinary measurement defects sufficed to fabricate, and ablations to
``confirm'', an entire false research narrative.
We release the protocol, statistical tooling, and per-run predictions.
