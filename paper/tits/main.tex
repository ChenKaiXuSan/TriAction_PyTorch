\begin{abstract}
Head movements are a primary observable of driver attention, and modern
cabins increasingly carry several synchronized cameras.  We study four-class
recognition of head-movement direction (left/right/down/up) from three cabin
views, reporting a systematic campaign of more than one hundred trained
models on a 22-driver cohort.  Our contributions are threefold.
(1)~\emph{Method}: a parameter-efficient multi-view architecture that keeps a
single Kinetics-pretrained video transformer frozen and learns only a small
cross-view token-attention module (11M trainable parameters, 4\% of one
finetuned backbone).  Under person-held-out evaluation it improves \mF{} by
$+6.3$ points over an equally pretrained single-view model and $+4.1$ over
trivial late fusion (both significant), halves the across-driver variance,
and under a nested, selection-free protocol its \micro{} advantage over the
single-view control grows to $+5.1$ points ($p{=}8.6\times10^{-12}$).
(2)~\emph{Measurement}: we document how two common protocol defects---a
video-level split that leaks driver identity into validation, and per-batch
averaging of epoch metrics---jointly fabricated a coherent but false
architecture narrative on this task; several ``ablation-validated''
conclusions, including our own earlier ones, reversed under a sound protocol.
(3)~\emph{Negative results}: an analytic head-pose feature stream carries
genuine, class-complementary signal in isolation, yet none of three injection
routes (token-, query-, or logit-level) survived robustness checks, and six
class-imbalance remedies failed to prevent rare-class collapse.  We release
the person-held-out protocol, paired statistical tooling, and all per-run
predictions to support measurement-first research on small-cohort driver
monitoring.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{D}{river}-monitoring systems (DMS) are becoming a regulatory
requirement in new vehicles, and consumer test protocols increasingly score
direct driver monitoring.  Head behaviour is among the most informative
observables such systems can rely on.  Mirror checks, shoulder checks, and
downward glances toward devices are all expressed as short, directed head
movements; their frequency and direction carry information about situational
awareness that steering- or lane-based proxies cannot provide.  Compared
with gaze estimation, head-movement classification is robust to eyewear,
sunglasses, and low light, and can be computed from standard cabin cameras
without per-driver calibration.

Modern cabins increasingly provide \emph{several} such cameras---behind the
steering column, in the A-pillars, near the rear-view mirror---raising a
natural question: how much does multi-view observation actually contribute
to recognizing driver head behaviour, and through which fusion mechanism?
Intuitively the answer should be ``a lot'': a leftward head turn is nearly
self-occluding in the left camera but fully visible from the right, so views
should be complementary.  Whether that intuition survives controlled
measurement is the empirical subject of this paper.

Answering the question rigorously is harder than it appears, for reasons that
are structural to driver-monitoring research rather than incidental to our
study.  Cohorts are small---recruiting, instrumenting, and recording drivers
is expensive---so models see only tens of identities.  Classes are heavily
imbalanced, because natural driving produces many more downward and leftward
glances than upward ones.  Each driver contributes many highly correlated
clips: the same person, seat position, clothing, and illumination recur
across hundreds of segments.  Consequently a split at the \emph{clip} or
\emph{video} level leaks identity: a model can score well by recognizing
\emph{who} is moving rather than \emph{how}.  Finally, evaluation sets are
small enough that single-split results carry confidence intervals wider than
the effects typically reported.  These conditions conspire to make
small-cohort studies vulnerable to false conclusions that survive
conventional ablation practice---because the ablations inherit the same
defective measurement.

This paper is therefore deliberately \emph{measurement-first}.  Our initial
campaign of 40+ runs, conducted under a conventional protocol (random
video-level split, default metric logging), produced what appeared to be a
well-supported progression of architectural improvements, each step
confirmed by ablations.  Auditing the protocol invalidated essentially all
of it.  We rebuilt the evaluation around person-held-out cross-validation
with paired statistical tests, re-ran every claim, and report what
survives---together with a controlled account of how the defective protocol
manufactured the earlier narrative, and with the negative results of our
attempts to exploit head-pose priors and to mitigate rare-class collapse.

Concretely, our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{\mvmin{}}, a minimal multi-view architecture: one frozen
Kinetics-pretrained ViViT encoder shared across views, a per-view token
compression to 17 tokens, and a small trainable transformer whose attention
spans views.  Under person-held-out evaluation it delivers significant
\mF{} and robustness gains over equally pretrained single-view and
late-fusion controls, and its advantage \emph{grows} under a stricter
nested protocol---the signature of a real effect rather than an artifact.
\item \textbf{A sound evaluation protocol} for small-cohort driver
monitoring---person-held-out $k$-fold with a nested selection-free variant,
epoch-accumulated metrics, and paired McNemar/bootstrap tests---released with
tooling, together with a quantified case study of how two silent protocol
defects fabricated a false state-of-the-art narrative.
\item \textbf{Documented negative results} on two fronts that the community
is likely to attempt: injecting analytic head-pose priors (the signal is
real, but no token-, query-, or logit-level route survived robustness
checks) and six remedies for rare-class collapse (all failed).  We argue
that identity-invariant representation, not fusion architecture, is the
binding constraint.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Driver monitoring: tasks and datasets}
Vision-based driver monitoring has been surveyed extensively in the context
of increasingly automated vehicles~\cite{ohnbar2016looking}.  The most
studied task is distracted-driving posture classification, from early
cabin-camera corpora~\cite{abouelnaga2018distracted} to the recent
100-Driver dataset~\cite{wang2023driver100}, which scales the cohort to one
hundred drivers precisely because cross-driver variation dominates smaller
corpora.  Fine-grained behaviour recognition is anchored by
Drive\&Act~\cite{martin2019driveact}, with five cabin views and a
hierarchical activity taxonomy, and DMD~\cite{ortega2020dmd}, with
multi-modal streams for attention and alertness analysis; both established
multi-camera cabins as the standard research setting and popularized
cross-subject splits.  Related lines include maneuver anticipation from
cabin and road views~\cite{jain2015brain4cars}, open-set driver anomaly
detection~\cite{kopuklu2021dad}, and drowsiness
detection~\cite{weng2016nthu}.  Across these efforts, cohort sizes of
10--70 drivers are the norm, and while subject-independent evaluation is
often reported, the \emph{interaction} of tiny cohorts, class imbalance,
and identity leakage---and its capacity to fabricate architecture-level
conclusions---has to our knowledge not been analyzed quantitatively in this
domain; that analysis (Sec.~\ref{sec:pitfalls}) is one of our
contributions.

\subsection{Head pose and head dynamics in the cabin}
Static head-pose estimation is a mature topic with dedicated benchmarks and
landmark-free regressors: random-forest methods on depth
data~\cite{fanelli2013biwi}, stage-wise soft aggregation in
FSA-Net~\cite{yang2019fsanet}, classification-regression hybrids such as
HopeNet~\cite{ruiz2018hopenet}, full-range extensions such as
WHENet~\cite{zhou2020whenet}, and rotation-representation refinements such
as 6DRepNet~\cite{hempel2022sixdrepnet}.  In the cabin, head pose commonly
serves as a proxy for gaze zones~\cite{vora2018gaze}, and head dynamics
have been used as an anticipatory cue for maneuvers~\cite{jain2015brain4cars}.
Our task differs in that we classify the \emph{direction of a spontaneous
head movement}---a temporal, behaviour-level label defined by motion rather
than by any static orientation; a per-frame pose estimate is neither the
input nor the output of our models.  We do, however, use analytically
computed pose trajectories as a diagnostic probe and as a candidate prior
(Sec.~\ref{sec:keypoints}), connecting the two lines: the probe shows the
pose signal is genuinely informative for movement direction on unseen
drivers, while our injection study shows that exploiting it inside a video
model is harder than the intuition suggests.

\subsection{Video backbones and parameter-efficient transfer}
Video classification progressed from 3-D
CNNs---I3D~\cite{carreira2017i3d}, 3-D
ResNets~\cite{hara2018spatiotemporal},
SlowFast~\cite{feichtenhofer2019slowfast}, and efficiency-oriented designs
such as X3D~\cite{feichtenhofer2020x3d}---to transformer architectures:
TimeSformer~\cite{bertasius2021timesformer}, ViViT~\cite{arnab2021vivit},
multiscale variants~\cite{fan2021mvit}, and shifted-window
designs~\cite{liu2022videoswin}, typically pretrained on
Kinetics~\cite{kay2017kinetics} or via masked
autoencoding~\cite{tong2022videomae}.  Benchmarks whose labels are defined
by motion rather than appearance, notably
Something-Something~\cite{goyal2017something}, are the closest analogue to
our direction-classification task.  For small downstream datasets, the
parameter-efficient transfer literature---adapters~\cite{houlsby2019adapters},
low-rank updates~\cite{hu2022lora}, visual prompt
tuning~\cite{jia2022vpt}, and video-specific variants such as
ST-Adapter~\cite{pan2022stadapter}, AIM~\cite{yang2023aim}, and light
decoders over entirely frozen encoders~\cite{lin2022frozenclip}---argues
that full finetuning is neither necessary nor optimal.  Our results give
this argument unusually sharp support at the extreme end of the data
spectrum: on 80 training videos, partial unfreezing of the backbone
contributed nothing measurable in any protocol we tested
(Sec.~\ref{sec:ablation}), while the frozen pretrained representation
itself contributed the single largest accuracy gain of the study
($+5.5$ \micro{} over a Kinetics-pretrained 3-D CNN).

\subsection{Multi-view action recognition and fusion}
Multi-view and cross-view action recognition has been driven by large
multi-camera corpora such as NTU~RGB+D~\cite{shahroudy2016ntu}, its 120-class
extension~\cite{liu2020ntu120}, and continuous multi-modal benchmarks such
as PKU-MMD~\cite{liu2017pkummd}, with fusion classically organized into
input-level (early), feature-level (mid), and decision-level (late)
strategies.  With transformer backbones, a natural mid-level operator is
token-space attention across views~\cite{vaswani2017attention}: each view's
tokens attend to every other view's, subsuming learned per-view weighting
and pairwise view alignment as special cases.  Most multi-view work reports
that fusion improves accuracy; far fewer studies control for the confound
that a stronger backbone, rather than the fusion itself, may carry the
gain.  Our contribution is such a controlled \emph{attribution}: with
backbone, pretraining, input sampling, and optimization held fixed, we
measure what cross-view token attention buys beyond single-view processing
and beyond trivial logit-mean late fusion on a small cohort---and find the
benefit real but located on the class-balance and robustness axes rather
than headline accuracy (Sec.~\ref{sec:main}).

\subsection{Class imbalance and long-tailed recognition}
Class imbalance is typically addressed by loss re-weighting and
margin-based designs---focal loss~\cite{lin2017focal}, class-balanced
loss~\cite{cui2019classbalanced}, label-distribution-aware
margins~\cite{cao2019ldam}---by distribution-aware calibration such as
balanced softmax~\cite{ren2020balanced} and logit
adjustment~\cite{menon2021logit}, or by decoupling representation and
classifier learning~\cite{kang2020decoupling}.  These techniques were
developed and validated predominantly on large-scale image benchmarks.  Our
study contributes a cautionary data point at the opposite extreme: on a
small-cohort video task evaluated subject-independently, every imbalance
remedy we tested was neutral or harmful (Sec.~\ref{sec:collapse}), and the
failure was not monotone in class frequency---the second-largest class
failed nearly as badly as the rarest---indicating that visual
confusability, not rarity alone, drives the collapse.  Transferring
long-tail machinery to this regime is an open problem.

\subsection{Evaluation methodology, leakage, and negative results}
The reliability of empirical conclusions in machine learning has been
questioned from several directions: dataset bias~\cite{torralba2011bias},
test-set overfitting and replication studies~\cite{recht2019imagenet,
beyer2020done}, run-to-run variance unaccounted for in
benchmarks~\cite{bouthillier2021accounting}, and information leakage as a
principal driver of a reproducibility crisis in applied
ML~\cite{kapoor2023leakage}.  Domain studies show the specific harm of
subject-dependent splits: record-wise instead of subject-wise
cross-validation inflates clinical prediction results~\cite{saeb2017needs},
and the same effect is documented for wearable activity
recognition~\cite{jordao2018har}.  Statistical practice for comparing
classifiers on shared test sets is long
established---McNemar's test~\cite{mcnemar1947}, Dietterich's
analysis~\cite{dietterich1998}, bootstrap confidence
intervals~\cite{efron1993bootstrap}---yet applied vision papers still
mostly report single-run point estimates, and community reproducibility
programs~\cite{pineau2021reproducibility} exist precisely to close this
gap.  We add to this literature a fully quantified case study in driver
monitoring in which two independent, individually mundane defects
interacted to produce a self-consistent false narrative---including a false
negative as well as false positives---and we normalize the reporting of
negative results for methods the community would plausibly retry.

\section{Task and Dataset}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Recording setup}

(day/night $\times$ high/low ambient light), yielding 88 sessions.  Three
synchronized cameras observe the cabin from the front, left, and right at
30\,fps and $332{\times}224$ resolution.  Sessions are several minutes
long; drivers perform natural sequences of head movements.

\subsection{Annotation}
Timeline annotations mark head-movement segments with one of eight direction
labels, merged to four classes---\emph{left}, \emph{right}, \emph{down},
\emph{up}---with vertical components taking precedence (\eg{} left-up
$\rightarrow$ up).  Unlabelled spans are excluded; there is no background
class.  Movements are short (median 1.03\,s), so the classifier input is a
segment clip, uniformly subsampled in time.

Three annotators labelled every session independently.  Frame-level
inter-annotator agreement, computed over 399k co-labelled frames, is 91.0\%
overall; per-class conditional agreement is 89\% (left), 94\% (right), 94\%
(down), and 70\% (up).  Two consequences matter for interpreting our
results.  First, the rare-class failures reported in
Sec.~\ref{sec:collapse}---in particular the near-total failure on
\emph{right}, the class with the \emph{highest} annotator agreement---cannot
be attributed to label ambiguity.  Second, \emph{up} is both the rarest and
the noisiest class; its results should be read with that compound difficulty
in mind.

\subsection{Why this task is statistically hostile}
\label{sec:hostile}
Three properties interact.
(i)~\emph{Small cohort}: 22 identities upper-bound what any split can test;
person-level generalization is the deployment-relevant question, and it can
only be measured by holding people out.
(ii)~\emph{Correlated clips}: each session yields tens of segments of the
same person in the same seat, so clip- or video-level splits measure
identity recall rather than behaviour recognition.  We later show
(Sec.~\ref{sec:pitfalls}) that the same model loses 6.6 \micro{} points when
identity leakage is removed.
(iii)~\emph{Imbalance}: predicting the majority class alone achieves 42.7\%
\micro{}; any method must be judged against that floor, and \micro{} alone
cannot expose collapse onto frequent classes.
A single 8-video validation split---the protocol of our own first
campaign---carries a binomial 95\% CI of $\pm6.4$ points and, as we later
measured, up to 15.6 points of across-fold spread.  Every architectural
effect we chased in four campaign iterations was smaller than that noise.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
We treat the protocol as a first-class contribution and specify it fully;
tooling is released with the paper.

\textbf{Person-held-out 5-fold CV.}  The 22 drivers are partitioned into
five folds (4--5 drivers each; strided assignment after a seeded shuffle, so
fold sizes differ by at most one driver).  For fold $k$, \emph{all} sessions
of the fold's drivers form the evaluation set; the model trains on the
remaining drivers' sessions.  Pooling the five folds scores every driver
exactly once with a model that never saw them, giving $n{=}3954$ evaluated
segments.  Table~\ref{tab:folds} lists the fold composition and illustrates
a fact central to this paper: fold difficulty varies enormously (majority
floors from 36\% to 53\%; arm-level \micro{} from 21\% to 66\% across arms
and folds), which is precisely why single-split results on this class of
data are uninterpretable.

\textbf{Nested selection-free variant.}  Checkpoint selection itself can
leak: choosing the best epoch on the same people one reports on inflated our
arms by $+0.9$ to $+2.0$ \micro{} points (measured by comparison against
fixed-epoch selection).  In the nested protocol, three drivers are further
carved out of each training set as an inner validation set; checkpoints are
selected on the inner set and reported on the held-out fold.  Train,
inner-validation, and test sets are person-disjoint by construction.

\textbf{Metrics.}  \micro{} (segment accuracy), macro-accuracy, and \mF{},
all \emph{accumulated over the full epoch} and reduced once
(Sec.~\ref{sec:pitfalls} quantifies why this matters).  We report \mF{}
alongside \micro{} because the majority class alone reaches 42.7\%; balance
across movement directions is the operationally relevant axis for a
monitoring system that must not miss rare checks.

\textbf{Statistics.}  All model comparisons are \emph{paired} on identical
segment sets---verified fold-by-fold by asserting label-sequence equality
before testing: McNemar's test with continuity
correction~\cite{mcnemar1947,dietterich1998} for \micro{}, and a paired
bootstrap (1{,}500--2{,}000 resamples, 95\% percentile
CIs)~\cite{efron1993bootstrap} for all metrics.  The pooled protocol's
binomial CI is $\pm1.6$ points; we treat sub-CI differences as noise
regardless of nominal $p$-values.

\textbf{Training.}  All arms: 50 epochs, no early stopping, Adam
(lr $10^{-4}$, cosine decay to zero), effective batch 16 (gradient
accumulation as needed), bf16 mixed precision, class-frequency weights in
the cross-entropy loss.  ViViT arms sample 32 frames at $224^2$; 3-D CNN
arms 8 frames.  One H100 GPU per run; a full five-fold arm trains in
15--60 GPU-hours depending on the architecture.

\section{Method: \mvmin{}}
\label{sec:method}
The architecture that survives evaluation is deliberately minimal
(Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}).  Let $V{=}\{\text{front},\text{left},\text{right}\}$
and let $x_v\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times T\times H\times W}$ be view $v$'s clip.

\textbf{Frozen shared encoder.}  A single ViViT-B/16$\times$2
encoder~\cite{arnab2021vivit} with factorized spatio-temporal attention,
pretrained on Kinetics-400~\cite{kay2017kinetics}, processes each view
independently with shared, frozen weights, producing token matrices
$Z_v\in\mathbb{R}^{(1+N_tN_s)\times D}$ with $N_t{=}16$ temporal tubelets,
$N_s{=}196$ spatial patches, and $D{=}768$.  The encoder runs under
\texttt{no\_grad}; no backbone parameter is updated at any point.

\textbf{Token compression.}  Attention over the full $3\times3137$ tokens is
computationally wasteful and, with 80 training videos, an overfitting
surface.  Per view we keep $1{+}N_t{=}17$ tokens: the CLS token and one
spatially averaged token per tubelet,
\begin{equation}
z_v^{(t)}=\frac{1}{N_s}\sum_{s=1}^{N_s}Z_v^{(t,s)},\qquad t=1,\dots,N_t,
\end{equation}
preserving the temporal axis (movements are defined by temporal structure)
while marginalizing space, which the frozen encoder has already summarized
into each tubelet's tokens.

\textbf{Cross-view fusion.}  Each view's tokens receive a learned additive
view embedding $e_v\in\mathbb{R}^{D}$, marking provenance.  The
$3\times17{=}51$ tokens are concatenated into one sequence and processed by
a 2-layer, 8-head transformer encoder~\cite{vaswani2017attention} (FFN
width 2048, dropout 0.1, pre-norm) whose self-attention spans all
views---so a front-view tubelet token can attend directly to the temporally
corresponding right-view token.  Mean pooling over the fused sequence and a
linear head produce the 4-class logits; training minimizes class-weighted
cross-entropy
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}=-\sum_{c} w_c\,\mathbb{1}[y{=}c]\,
\log p_\theta\!\left(c\,\middle|\,\{x_v\}_{v\in V}\right),
\end{equation}
with $w_c$ inverse-frequency class weights.

\textbf{Capacity and cost.}  Trainable parameters: 11M (fusion module and
head), \ie{} 4\% of one finetuned ViViT-B.  The frozen encoder dominates
inference cost and is shared, so the multi-view model costs three encoder
passes plus a negligible 51-token transformer; peak training memory is
1.4\,GB (frozen backbone under \texttt{no\_grad}) versus 6\,GB with partial
finetuning.

Three design decisions deserve emphasis because each contradicts what our
own leaky-protocol ablations had ``established'': the backbone is fully
frozen (partial unfreezing measured n.s.\ under the sound protocol, and its
apparent $+2.9$ was an artifact); no keypoint stream is included
(Sec.~\ref{sec:keypoints}); and aggregation is plain mean pooling (learned
gated aggregation hurt in every protocol we tried).

system, missing rare-but-critical movements (\emph{up}: mirror checks) and
failing on unfamiliar drivers are the relevant failure modes; multi-view
observation demonstrably helps on exactly these axes.

best fusion of our leaky-protocol campaign (48.9 vs.\ 37.2 single view),

merely shrink---they changed sign.

\section{Head-Pose Priors: Real Signal, No Robust Injection Route}
\label{sec:keypoints}
Head movements should be readable from head pose, and the scenario provides
per-frame 3-D keypoints.  This section documents why naive keypoint fusion
failed, evidence that the pose signal itself is real and complementary, and
the failure of three injection routes---reported so that others need not
rediscover them.

\subsection{Why raw keypoint streams fail}
The raw $70{\times}3$ keypoint tensor is dominated by hands: 42 of 70 rows
(60\% of input dimensions) are hand points whose frame-to-frame motion is
$4\times$ that of the head, leaving the head with roughly 3\% of the input's
motion energy.  Coordinates are unnormalized camera coordinates, so the
largest-variance directions encode \emph{who and where the driver
is}---seat position, body size---precisely the identity signal a
person-held-out model must ignore.  And the earlier design compressed the
whole trajectory into a single token competing with 51 video tokens, which
attention can trivially ignore.  The head signal never actually reached the
model; the corresponding arms measured n.s.\ accordingly
(Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).

\subsection{Analytic head-pose features}
We compute what the network was supposed to learn.  With $p^{(i)}_t$ the
3-D position of keypoint $i$ at frame $t$, define the ear midpoint
$o_t{=}\tfrac12(p^{\mathrm{le}}_t{+}p^{\mathrm{re}}_t)$, the ear axis
$\mathbf{e}_t{=}\overline{p^{\mathrm{le}}_t{-}p^{\mathrm{re}}_t}$, and the
facing direction $\mathbf{f}_t$ as the nose direction orthogonalized
against $\mathbf{e}_t$ (bars denote unit normalization).  Head angles
follow analytically:
\begin{align}
\mathrm{yaw}_t &= \operatorname{atan2}\!\big(f_{t,x},\,|f_{t,z}|\big),
\nonumber\\
\mathrm{pitch}_t &= \operatorname{atan2}\!\big(f_{t,y},\,
\lVert f_{t,xz}\rVert\big),\\
\mathrm{roll}_t &= \operatorname{atan2}\!\big(e_{t,y},\,
\lVert e_{t,xz}\rVert\big).\nonumber
\end{align}
The per-frame feature (33-D) concatenates the three angles, their first
differences (the label is a \emph{movement}---a derivative), the
head-centroid velocity, and the eight head-related points expressed in the
local frame $(\mathbf{e}_t,\mathbf{f}_t,\mathbf{e}_t{\times}\mathbf{f}_t)$.
Angles and local coordinates are centred by their per-segment temporal
mean, removing each driver's resting posture (identity) while preserving
the movement.  Unit tests verify that the features recover synthetic
rotations exactly and are invariant to whole-head translation.

\subsection{The signal is real}
A 64-unit MLP trained on these features alone---no video---reaches
27.2 \mF{} on unseen drivers with \emph{non-zero F$_1$ on every class}:
right 0.24 and up 0.12, where every video model scores ${\approx}0.03$ and
$0.00$ (Table~\ref{tab:collapse}).  Within a clip, yaw spans up to
$69^\circ$.  The prior is strong, cheap, and complementary to the video
models' failure modes---exactly the profile fusion research hopes for.

\subsection{Three injection routes, none robust}
\textbf{Token-level.}  Per-frame pose tokens (one per frame per view,
projected to $D$, tagged with a stream embedding) initially produced the
best \micro{} of the campaign (53.3, $+1.7$ over \mvmin{},
$p{=}8.9\times10^{-5}$).  Three pre-registered robustness checks demoted
it: seed sensitivity ($+1.7/+3.3/+0.4$ across seeds; significant in 2 of
3); \emph{reversal} under the nested protocol ($-2.5$,
$p{=}3.2\times10^{-7}$, Table~\ref{tab:nested}), showing part of the gain
came from checkpoint selection on scored drivers; and a representation
control in which raw flattened keypoints in the same per-frame token format
captured most of the effect ($+1.1$, $p{=}0.021$), leaving the analytic
encoding itself at $+0.6$ (n.s.).
\textbf{Query-level.}  Using the pose trajectory as the cross-attention
query for pooling: $+1.3$ \micro{} ($p{=}0.004$) but \mF-neutral, and not
robust under nesting (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).
\textbf{Logit-level.}  A pre-registered 50/50 probability average of the
pose-only probe and the video model degraded \micro{} by $9.1$ points
($p{=}5.4\times10^{-30}$): the probe is 22 points weaker overall, and the
equal-weight blend loses more on frequent classes than it recovers on rare
ones.  An $\alpha$-sweep shows a mild trade-off region near $\alpha{=}0.8$
(up-F$_1$ $0.02{\to}0.06$ at approximately flat \micro), which we report as
sensitivity only: selecting $\alpha$ on test folds would reintroduce
selection bias.

The open problem is thus precise: an injection mechanism that preserves the
probe's rare-class competence without surrendering the video model's
frequent-class strength.  Learning the blend weight---or a
class-conditional gate---on nested inner-validation drivers is the
legitimate next step; we release the aligned per-segment predictions of
both models to enable it.

Two failure modes define the actual difficulty of this task.

\textbf{Collapse.}  The confusion matrix (Table~\ref{tab:confusion}) is
blunt: \emph{right} recall is 14\% with 41\% of right segments predicted as
\emph{left}, and \emph{up} recall is 5\%---despite 94\% annotator agreement
on \emph{right}.  The pattern is identical in every video architecture we
trained, from 3-D CNNs to every ViViT variant, which rules out
architecture-specific explanations.  Six remedies spanning loss design
(class weighting~\cite{cui2019classbalanced}, focal
loss~\cite{lin2017focal}, balanced softmax~\cite{ren2020balanced}), input
design (clip-mean subtraction, intended to delete static appearance), and
fusion design (pose tokens, logit fusion) all failed
(Table~\ref{tab:remedies}); balanced softmax equalized the prediction
distribution at the cost of a collapse to 26.4\% \micro.  Notably, the
failure is not symmetric in frequency: \emph{right} (19.8\% of data) fails
almost as badly as \emph{up} (8.3\%), suggesting the problem is not purely
statistical rarity but a genuine visual confusability of horizontal
directions under our camera geometry, compounded by imbalance.

\textbf{Driver memorisation.}  Under person hold-out, the best checkpoint
lands at epoch 0--4 in \emph{every} arm: models begin memorising training
drivers almost immediately, consistent with the large across-driver spread
(fold-level \micro{} from 21\% to 63\% for the 3-D CNN baseline).
Clip-mean subtraction, our direct attack on static appearance, removed
useful signal along with identity ($-7.9$).  We conclude that
identity-invariant representation---not fusion architecture---is the
binding constraint for small-cohort driver monitoring, and nominate it as
the field's priority.

\section{How a Defective Protocol Fabricated a Narrative}
\label{sec:pitfalls}
We document this episode quantitatively because both defects are common,
silent, individually plausible-looking, and mutually reinforcing.

\textbf{Defect 1: identity leakage.}  Our first campaign split at the video
level; all seven validation drivers also appeared in training.  Scores
measured recall of known drivers.

\textbf{Defect 2: per-batch metric averaging.}  Epoch metrics were computed
per batch (size 2) and averaged---at that batch size \mF{} is inflated by
up to 17 points (0.52 logged vs.\ 0.35 recomputed)---and, worse,
\emph{inconsistently}: one trainer family accumulated correctly, so
cross-architecture comparisons silently mixed two incompatible scales.

on scored drivers inflated arms by $+0.9$ to $+2.0$ points.  Nothing about
these defects is exotic---one is a dataloader default, the other a
metric-API misuse---which is precisely why we report the case
quantitatively and release the protocol and tooling as the antidote.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}
\textbf{What multi-view is for.}  On this task, multi-view observation does
not raise headline accuracy over a strong pretrained single-view model; it
raises class balance ($+6.3$ \mF) and cross-driver stability (spread
halved), and its advantage grows under the strictest protocol.  Deployments
should choose multi-view for robustness on rare safety-relevant movements
and unfamiliar drivers---not for benchmark accuracy.

\textbf{What the field should prioritize.}  Every video model we trained
memorises drivers within a few epochs and collapses on horizontal-direction
rare classes; no loss-, input-, or fusion-level remedy we tested repaired
this.  Identity-invariant representation learning---person-adversarial
objectives, pose-anchored normalization, or pretraining across much larger
driver populations---is in our reading the binding constraint, ahead of
fusion architecture.

\textbf{Limitations.}  (i)~One cohort, one vehicle type, 22 drivers: the
regime we analyze is exactly the regime our conclusions cover;
cross-dataset validation on Drive\&Act~\cite{martin2019driveact} and
DMD~\cite{ortega2020dmd} is future work.  (ii)~Segment-level classification
with oracle temporal boundaries sidesteps detection; an online system must
also localize movements.  (iii)~The keypoint study depends on one 3-D body
estimator; head-dedicated pose estimators~\cite{ruiz2018hopenet,
zhou2020whenet} may change the injection results.  (iv)~Our negative
results are task-scoped: we show these remedies fail here, not everywhere.
(v)~With 22 drivers, even person-held-out results retain cohort-level
uncertainty that only larger populations can remove.

\textbf{Recommendations for small-cohort DMS research.}
(1)~Split by person; report pooled person-held-out results with per-fold
spread.  (2)~Select checkpoints on person-disjoint inner validation.
(3)~Accumulate metrics over the epoch; verify metric parity across
baselines.  (4)~Pair every claim with McNemar/bootstrap statistics on
identical samples.  (5)~Report \mF{} beside \micro{} whenever a majority
class exceeds a third of the data.  (6)~Publish per-run predictions so
later work can re-test claims without retraining.

\section{Conclusion}
Under person-held-out, statistically tested evaluation of more than twenty
experimental arms (100+ trained models), the defensible findings on
multi-view driver head-movement recognition are:
(1)~Kinetics-pretrained video transformers dominate accuracy
($+5.5$ \micro{} over a pretrained 3-D CNN);
(2)~a frozen-backbone cross-view token-attention module with 4\% of the
trainable parameters of one finetuned backbone adds class balance
($+6.3$ \mF), halves across-driver spread, and, under the strictest
selection-free protocol, adds $+5.1$ \micro{} ($p{=}8.6\times10^{-12}$)
over its single-view control;
(3)~head-pose priors carry real, class-complementary signal, but no
injection route yet survives robustness checks;
(4)~rare-class collapse and driver memorisation, not fusion architecture,
are the binding constraints---six remedies failed;
(5)~two ordinary measurement defects sufficed to fabricate, and ablations
to ``confirm,'' an entire false research narrative.
We release the protocol, statistical tooling, and per-run predictions.
